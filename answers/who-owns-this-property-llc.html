Who owns a property held by an LLC?

Start with the county's official records. The appraiser lists the LLC as titleholder; the deed in the clerk's records names the signer; and the state corporate registry lists that company's managers, officers, and registered agent. Cross-reference those names and the same person's other companies, and the individual behind the entity usually resolves in three lookups.

Why is the owner an LLC in the first place?

Entity ownership is ordinary, not evasive. Liability separation, lender requirements, partnership structures, and estate planning all push commercial property into single-purpose entities. A serious owner may hold twenty parcels in twenty companies.

The consequence for anyone sourcing deals is the same either way: the name on the tax roll is a company that cannot answer a phone, and the parcel looks unconnected to every other parcel that owner controls.

Which records actually name a human?

Three, in this order:

  1. The property appraiser's roll gives you the titleholder and the mailing address — often the owner's operating office rather than the property. County property appraiser, real property roll
  2. The recorded deed in the clerk's official records names the grantor, the grantee, and — critically — the person who signed for the entity, with the witnesses and notary. Clerk of the circuit court, official records
  3. The state corporate registry lists the LLC's managers or managing members, its officers, its registered agent, and its annual filing history. Florida Division of Corporations (Sunbiz), annual reports and entity filings

The mailing address is the most underrated field on the list. Shared addresses across otherwise unrelated entities are the fastest signal that one operator stands behind all of them.

How do you connect one person to their other entities?

Once you have a human name, run it back through the corporate registry to find every company that names the same person as manager or officer. Then map those companies back onto the parcel roll.

  • Shared officers cluster entities that share a manager, member, or officer name.
  • Shared mailing addresses catch the entities whose officer names differ but whose office does not.
  • Name normalisation matters: registry names and tax-roll names are formatted differently, so "SMITH, JOHN A" and "John A. Smith" must resolve to one person.

The output is a portfolio: every parcel one operator controls in the county, regardless of how many companies it is spread across.

What stops the trail?

Some structures do not resolve to a person from public records alone:

  • Entities managed by another entity, sometimes chained across states.
  • Out-of-state or offshore parents whose home registry discloses less.
  • Trusts, where the trustee is named but the beneficiary is not.
  • Professional registered-agent services, which are designed to end the trail.

Treat these as a signal in their own right. An owner who has built four layers between themselves and the tax roll is telling you something about how they will negotiate.

How does parcelrow do this at county scale?

The same three records, joined for every parcel in the county rather than one at a time. Corporate filings are matched to the tax roll, officers are clustered across entities, and the result is an owner graph you can click: select a parcel, see the operator behind it, and see every other parcel they hold. Terms used here

Registered agent
The person or firm designated to receive legal service for an entity. Frequently a commercial service, in which case it says nothing about ownership.
Managing member
The member of an LLC with authority to act for it. Listed on the state annual report, and usually the person who signs a deed.
Official records
The county clerk's recorded document index — deeds, mortgages, liens — cited by book and page or by instrument number.
Grantor and grantee
The party conveying an interest and the party receiving it. On an entity-held parcel, the signature block beneath the grantee names a human.

Common questions

Is looking up the person behind an LLC legal?
Yes. Every source in this path is a public record: the property appraiser's roll, the clerk's recorded deeds, and the state corporate registry. Parcelrow uses only public records and cites each one at the source.
What if the registered agent is a law firm or a registered-agent service?
Then the agent tells you nothing about ownership, which is the point of using one. Skip to the manager and officer names on the annual report, and to the signer on the recorded deed — those are the people who act for the entity.
Why does an owner's other property matter?
Because it tells you who you are actually calling. An owner with eleven parcels on the same corridor is a consolidator with a strategy; an owner with one inherited lot is a different conversation entirely.
